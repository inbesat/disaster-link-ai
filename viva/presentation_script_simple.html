Arnav ~2:45

When the Ganga river floods in Bihar, the first thing that happens is the cell phone towers stop working. In the last big flood, more than half the villages lost phone signal within the first few hours. Think about that — the people who need help the most, the farmers in low-lying villages, the families who only have basic feature phones, the elderly who don't use smartphones — they're the ones who get cut off completely.

Most disaster apps today assume you have a smartphone and a good internet connection. They show you a risk score on a map and that's it. But when the network dies, that map is useless. SafeSphere is built for that exact moment — when the internet is gone but people still need to know what to do.

Our solution is simple: we built one platform that works as two completely different apps. If you're a citizen, you get a simple emergency app — it shows your risk level, the nearest shelter, one big SOS button, and lets you tell your family you're safe. If you're a government responder, you get a full emergency command center — live maps, AI-powered evacuation plans, resource tracking, and the ability to send alerts through nine different channels. Same data, same system, but each person sees exactly what they need.

Here's what makes us different — five things no other disaster app does:

First, we don't just predict floods — we plan evacuations. Our AI creates a detailed 48-hour plan: which villages to evacuate first, which shelters have space, what resources to send where, and the exact timeline.

Second, we reach people with zero internet. We send alerts through nine channels at once — SMS, phone notifications, email, WhatsApp, and critically, FM radio. We generate a Hindi voice message, wrap it in the official emergency radio format, and push it to radio stations in eight seconds. Even someone with an old feature phone or a car radio gets the warning.

Third, our AI doesn't make things up. Every recommendation is backed by real Indian disaster guidelines — the NDMA rules, district plans, official procedures. When the AI says "evacuate this village via this route to this shelter," it cites the exact guideline section it's drawn from.

Fourth, the whole thing works offline. The app caches 48 hours of data on your phone — maps, shelter locations, alerts, predictions. There's even a small AI model that runs right in your browser with no internet needed. And if even that fails, we have 61 pre-written emergency answers for common questions.

Fifth, strict security. Citizens can never see government data. Government users only see their own district. Three layers of protection make sure of that.

Predict. Plan. Alert. Evacuate. Verify. One platform from the river gauge to the field responder.

Tavneet & Vedanjali ~3:00

Tavneet: Let me start with what you see and interact with. We built the app using Next.js — a modern web framework that loads pages instantly because it does the heavy work on our servers first, then sends you the finished page. This is crucial in disaster zones where internet is slow. We use React for the interactive parts, and TypeScript — which is like JavaScript but with a safety net that catches errors before they reach users.

For the look and feel, we use Tailwind CSS — it lets us build consistent, good-looking interfaces fast. The maps run on MapLibre, which is like Google Maps but completely free and open-source. It draws maps using your phone's graphics chip, so it's smooth even with thousands of markers. We also use a library called Turf.js that does map math right on your phone — calculating distances, drawing safety zones, checking if a location is inside a flood area — all without needing internet.

The database is PostgreSQL on Supabase — a rock-solid database system used by companies worldwide. We added two special extensions: PostGIS for geographic data (so we can ask "what's within 5 km of here?" and get answers in milliseconds), and pgvector for AI search (so we can find relevant emergency documents by meaning, not just keywords). We use Prisma as our database tool — it writes the SQL for us and catches mistakes automatically.

For offline use, we use something called IndexedDB — it's a database built into every browser. We wrap it with a tool called Dexie that makes it easy to use. Your phone stores shelter data, predictions, alerts, and pending reports locally. A background script called a Service Worker syncs everything back to the server when internet returns.

Vedanjali: Now the AI side. You've probably heard about "AI agents." Ours are different — the five agents that create evacuation plans are not chatbots. They're step-by-step logical pipelines: one assesses flood risk, one plans evacuation routes, one assigns resources like boats and medical kits, one checks for conflicts like overcrowded shelters. They use keywords and rules, not AI guesswork, so they can't hallucinate wrong evacuation orders. Only the chat feature uses a large language model.

That chat has a chain of six fallback AI providers — if one is down or out of credits, it automatically tries the next. The last resort is a tiny AI model that runs entirely in your browser, and if even that fails, 61 pre-written emergency answers. The alert system that sends SMS, push notifications, and FM radio alerts never uses AI at all — it's pure logic, so it works even if every AI service is down.

Our flood prediction uses a machine learning model called XGBoost. It takes four inputs — how much rain fell in the last 3 days, whether the river is rising or falling, how wet the soil is, and the elevation of the land — and outputs a risk level. This runs as a separate Python service. [Note: The trained model file isn't in the repo yet, so the demo shows a placeholder prediction.]

For the knowledge base, we use a technique called RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation. We upload PDFs of NDMA guidelines and district disaster plans, break them into chunks, convert each chunk to a list of numbers that represents its meaning, and store them in the database. When someone asks a question, we find the most relevant chunks and give them to the AI as context. If the AI service is unavailable, we fall back to simple keyword search — the AI still gets useful context, just not as smart.

Anticipated Q&A — simple answers for any judge

Q: "Are these really AI agents?"

A: The planning agents are logical pipelines — they follow rules and keywords, not AI guessing. Only the chat uses a large language model. Critical decisions like evacuation orders can't be hallucinated because they're rule-based.

Q: "Is the flood prediction real or fake?"

A: The model is real — it takes 4 measurements and outputs risk. But the trained model file isn't in our demo, so we show a placeholder. We're transparent: we'll say "demo prediction" on stage.

Q: "What if all your AI services go down?"

A: The alert system (SMS, push, FM radio) never uses AI — it's pure logic, so it always works. The chat falls through 6 AI providers, then a browser AI, then 61 pre-written answers. The knowledge base falls back to keyword search. Everything degrades gracefully — nothing crashes.

Q: "How does offline work?"

A: Real offline, not simulated. Your browser has a built-in database (IndexedDB). We store shelter data, predictions, alerts, pending reports there. A background script syncs when internet returns. Maps cache tiles you've viewed. There's even a small AI model that runs in the browser.

Q: "9 alert channels — which actually work?"

A: SMS (Twilio), phone notifications, email, FM radio (5 methods: direct API, car radio text, audio file upload, email to studio, phone call to control room), in-app alerts, WhatsApp (webhook ready). All fire in parallel. FM needs station partnerships for full end-to-end.

Q: "Is the knowledge base really using NDMA documents?"

A: Yes — we upload PDFs, extract text, chunk it, convert to meaning-vectors, store in the database with a fast search index. If the AI embedding service is down, we fall back to keyword search. Demo uses seeded NDMA guideline chunks.

Q: "How do you stop citizens from seeing government data?"

A: Three layers: 1) Middleware checks every request and redirects wrong-role users. 2) Server actions verify role before any changes. 3) Database itself enforces row-level security — users literally cannot query rows outside their district even if they hack the API.

Q: "What about all those NASA/GLOFAS/IMD environment variables?"

A: Of ~40 config variables, only ~20 are actually used in code (Supabase, weather, AI providers, SMS, push, text-to-speech). The others like NASA, GLOFAS, Sentinel Hub are declared for future integration but not wired up. We don't claim live integration with them.

Q: "Can this work outside Patna?"

A: Yes — the architecture is district-agnostic. Geographic queries, AI search, FM radio, offline database, planning agents — all parameterized by district. Deploy to another district by changing the data: shelters, resources, radio stations, alert rules.

Q: "Biggest technical risk?"

A: The trained flood model file isn't checked in — the prediction service shows a placeholder until we run training. Also, the app bundle is 6 MB which may load slowly on stage Wi-Fi. Both fixable before demo.
